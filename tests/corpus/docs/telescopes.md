# Telescopes

A second control document, unrelated to the service and to the other control.

## Refractors and reflectors

A refractor bends light through a lens; a reflector gathers it with a curved
mirror. Galileo's instruments were refractors, and their chromatic aberration —
the failure of a single lens to bring every wavelength to the same focus — is why
large modern instruments are all mirrors. A mirror reflects every colour
identically, and it can be supported across its whole back surface, so it can be
made far larger than a lens that must be held only at its rim.

## Aperture and resolution

Aperture governs both light-gathering and resolving power. Doubling the diameter
quadruples the collected light and halves the smallest resolvable angle. In
practice atmospheric turbulence limits ground-based resolution to about one
arcsecond regardless of aperture, which is why adaptive optics — deforming a
small mirror hundreds of times a second to cancel the atmosphere — matters more
than raw size past a certain point.

## Mounts

An equatorial mount has one axis aligned with the Earth's rotation, so tracking a
star is a single constant-rate motion. An altazimuth mount is mechanically
simpler and scales to much larger instruments, but tracking requires coordinated
motion on both axes and the field of view rotates as the object moves, which has
to be corrected optically for long exposures.

## Observing

Let the instrument cool to ambient temperature before observing; a warm mirror
sets up convection currents in the tube that smear the image as badly as poor
seeing. Dark adaptation takes about twenty minutes and a single glance at a white
light undoes it. Faint extended objects are best found with low magnification and
averted vision — looking slightly to one side of the target places its light on
the more sensitive periphery of the retina.
